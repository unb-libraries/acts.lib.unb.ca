ACTS Revival 2026!

The Atlantic Canadian Theatre Site (ACTS) features a digital archive of performance material from what is now known as “Atlantic Canada” from 1700 to 1900. The archive, which contains over 10,000 documents, includes production posters, playbills, performance calendars, and original prologues performed at specific productions.

Performance continues to play a crucial role in this area and ACTS aims to foster further research in performance histories. Although this project engages “new” media and performance, often digital archives are a vanguard of the old and remediate an already established canon in projects (Roopika Risam). This archive contains documented performance artifacts and acknowledges the inherited dominance of settler-colonial perspectives.

History

ACTS was founded by UNB English professor Edward Mullaly in the mid-1990s as an “academically-refereed Internet site which publishes a wide range of primary research materials of interest to Canadian theatre scholars, and to social, cultural, and political historians of the Atlantic region.[…] The primary focus of ACTS is to make such research available in a form which takes advantage of the searchability of electronic materials and thus allows for a re-viewing and restructuring of ‘old’ data in new ways” (“General”). ACTS eventually accrued an editorial board consisting of Mullaly, along with John Ball, Bruce Barton, Richard Plant, and Mary Elizabeth Smith. Most of the material on ACTS was collected by Mount St. Vincent English professor Patrick O’Neill, Ed Mullaly, and Anton Wagner.

The intention and scope of ACTS in the 1990s was at once noble and practical. By scanning and collecting as many primary source documents that fit its mission, anyone with access to the World Wide Web from the mid-1990s until the late-2000-aughts could search for what eventually amounted to thousands of records and several thousand images—including theatre listings, reviews, original scholarship, and playscripts—pertaining to theatre in the four Atlantic provinces and the three prairie provinces, as well as Ontario. All records were searchable through keywords.
